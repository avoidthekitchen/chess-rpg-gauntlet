# Story and recurring characters

[Design overview](../design.md) · [Campaign and curriculum](campaign.md) · [Journey rules](systems.md) · [Names and terms](glossary.md)

## Setting

**Setting:** an animal kingdom of ancient magic, told like a storybook. Each piece type is a species whose body hints at how it moves: frog knights hop over pieces, tortoise rooks carry a castle on their backs. Each enemy faction is made of its own creatures, such as the Bog Goblins' rats, crows and toads, and wears the same chess headgear as ours. The final villain is the Lich King, who leads skeleton versions of every species (a light wink at Lichess).

## Main story arc

Approved 2026-10-22. The theme comes straight from pillar 4, "mistakes are cheap, lessons are clear": the Lich King is a king who could never accept losing a game. The player wins by learning from every loss.

- **The premise.** You are the young Lion King of Pawnshire. Long ago, the former Lion King of Pawnshire refused to accept checkmate in the one game that mattered. He tipped over the board, swore he would never lose again, and his refusal outlived him: he became the Lich King. Now he wants the crown back. He doesn't attack you himself; he stirs up, hires or corrupts his neighbours one by one, and in the end even the kingdom's own army. The kingdom's old army is scattered, so you set out with a trusted companion. Published boards depict encounters along the journey; they are not your persistent roster.
- **The rule of the world.** Even the Lich King plays by the rules of chess on the board. That's the one law magic can't break, and it's why every battle is real chess. What he twists is people: greed, fear, pride, discipline, death. Each faction's voice and story show the flaw he exploits. Its pieces still follow the published puzzle replies.
- **Act structure.** Each Region is a chapter in four beats:
  1. **Arrival:** the faction's trouble.
  2. **Lessons:** short encounters that teach the chapter’s ideas.
  3. **Review:** a mixed challenge framed by the faction’s leader.
  4. **Turn:** the chapter resolves, another clue appears, and an ally may join the story.

| Region | Faction | What the Lich King uses | Chapter | Hook into the next Region |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Bog Goblins | Greed: whispers that the kingdom's villages are easy pickings | Grubnak raids the marsh villages; you take back the Bog and free Saffron from his pantry | Saffron says the Lich King sends his bandits next (already in the game) |
| 2 | Bandit Riders | Coin: the Lich King pays them to steal the old army's banners | Bandits strike from ambush along the roads; you recover the banners and the army starts to regroup | The bandits' paymaster wears the Shadow Cult's crystal |
| 3 | Shadow Cult | Fear: the cult promises its followers they'll never lose anything again | The cult binds villages with pins and spells; you break the binding, and the cult's records confirm who the Lich King was | The cult's records name a traitor general in the Iron Legion |
| 4 | Iron Legion | Pride: the kingdom's own disciplined army, turned by a general who thinks you're too young to rule | A war of sieges and open files; you win the Legion back rather than destroy it, and its general becomes an ally | The Legion's scouts report the Bone Wardens massing at the border |
| 5 | Bone Wardens | Death: the Lich's own vanguard of skeleton animals | An endgame chapter of small elite armies; you reach the gates of the Lich's keep | The keep's gate opens |
| Final | The Lich King | His refusal to lose | A mixed capstone against the king who never accepted defeat; he finally accepts the outcome | The story ends; earlier chapters remain replayable |

- **Who he was.** A former Lion King of Pawnshire: the player's mirror, the king who never learned from a loss. Whether he's your ancestor stays unsaid.
- **His origin comes in pieces.** One hint per Region from Region 2 on; the Shadow Cult's records confirm it in Region 3. No scene explains it all at once.
- **Defeated leaders can return, lightly.** Grubnak may pop up later as comic relief; the Iron Legion's general becomes an ally after Region 4.
- **Interludes are short.** One or two lines from the Lich King at the end of each Region.

## Recurring characters

**Your chosen companion coaches every lesson**, whichever piece moves. Sir Hob, Sage Ollie, and Old Brick keep their established voices.
Other pieces add brief reactions. A named companion is not automatically every piece of its type on a published board.

Saffron, Tadwell, and later allies can join scenes and the travelling cast without changing a roster or giving powers.
Their planned story roles do not require hiring, XP, stat upgrades, or matching pieces in each puzzle.
The table records established roles, not a claim that every character and scene is already implemented.

**Naming conventions.** Supporting pieces may have short, earthy names (Pip, Nib, Bramble, Granite, Brother Hoot). Heroes get one memorable name and a title ("Saffron, the Fox Queen"). Bosses get a name and a rank ("Grubnak, the Goblin Chief"). Every name is unique across the cast, so a supporting piece never shares a hero's name. Species and piece names are in [Names and terms](glossary.md).

Region-specific characters and story beats belong in their Region documents.

## Voice and dialogue

How every line in the game should read, drawn from what works in Region 1:

- **Short.** A talking-head quip during battle is one sentence, ideally under 12 words. A battle's tip and its dialogue lines are 1 or 2 sentences each, with 2 to 4 lines before the battle and 1 or 2 after. A boss or hero scene can run to 3 or 4 lines.
- **Plain chess words, one image.** Companions teach with real terms ("guard it", "count attackers and defenders") plus at most one storybook image ("a wall of pawns can't move backwards"). No notation in dialogue except square names when pointing at the board.
- **Teach, don't lecture.** The tip at the start of a battle is one actionable idea. The explanation lives in the worked example and journal.
- **Call back.** In later battles, the companion recalls the earlier lesson an idea came from ("Remember the hare's leap?"), so the spiral is visible.
- **Gentle humor, never mean.** Enemies can be greedy, pompous or silly, and may gloat over a capture ("Should've guarded it, hee!"), but never insult the player. Losing is never mocked; the review and the companion are encouraging.
- **Each faction sounds different.** Goblins are loud and greedy. Bandits are smooth and calculating. Cultists are hushed and certain. Their voices do not imply special move rules.
- **No lore dumps.** Story comes in one-line hints between battles and short scenes at Region ends. If a line explains history, cut it in half.
- **Our own words.** Lessons may use Lichess positions, but the text is always the companion's own voice, never Lichess's wording.

## Story and published boards

Story frames the journey; it does not change FENs, piece counts, colors, or replies to fit a scene.
Write board-specific dialogue only after inspecting the source. Never promise an enemy move outside the published line.
On mixed reviews, keep titles, intros, and initial dialogue neutral about the tactic.
Correct chess terminology takes priority over a joke. A missed puzzle is an opportunity to try again, never a narrative punishment.

The chapter arc is approved narrative direction. Missing interludes and ally scenes remain content work, not implemented mechanics.
